Architecture design · draft 6

Restaurant POS Architecture

A point of sale for one full-service Chinese restaurant with a sushi bar, a full bar and a catering business today, and a couple dozen locations later. Every location keeps selling when the internet drops, and keeps selling through the cloud when both of its local servers die.

Prepared for Mike29 Oct 2026Status: phase 1 in progress; draft 6 brings Android screens back next to macOS and iOS

1Summary & decisions

Five ideas carry the whole design.

  1. Terminals are screens. The Flutter app draws screens, collects what was chosen, and sends it as a small, append-only operation to a backend. It holds no business rules and no business data.
  2. One backend, two homes. All rules, including the menu engine, live in one Python backend. It runs on a pair of hubs at each location and in the cloud in two regions. Each side is a complete system on its own, both are live at all times, and a terminal uses whichever it can reach.
  3. Hubs are for speed and independence. They sit on the restaurant's own network, so they are fast, reach printers and kitchen screens without NAT or firewall tricks, keep that network closed to the internet, and keep the location working when the internet line is down.
  4. Orders are logs that merge. An order is the set of operations made against it. Two copies merge by taking the union, so hubs, cloud and terminals always agree, whatever order things arrive in.
  5. Everything else is data. Menus, taxes, languages, roles, routing and integrations are rows with effective dates, set per location in the admin portal. Opening in a new state or adding a provider is setup, not code.

2Stack

Screens are Apple devices (macOS and iOS) and Android phones and tablets. Windows, Linux and web screens stay out of scope. Android came back on 29 Oct 2026 for cheaper tablets and handhelds, built-in NFC and Android kitchen displays; the price is a second device-management system and an Android device in every hardware test. Flutter builds all three from one codebase with documentation among the best of any framework, and keeps the door open to other platforms later without a rewrite. Python with FastAPI fits the menu engine design as written and has the strongest tools for validation, migrations, spreadsheet import, testing and reporting. The split is strict: the app renders and collects, the backend prices, validates, routes and records.

  • Backend: FastAPI and Pydantic, SQLAlchemy and Alembic, Hypothesis for property tests, openpyxl for the spreadsheet round-trip. One container image runs on hubs and in the cloud.
  • Data: PostgreSQL on every hub and in the cloud with one schema, so the menu engine's JSONB, arrays and trigram search work unchanged. Cloud Postgres is managed (RDS or Cloud SQL) with point-in-time recovery. Terminals keep only settings and a short queue of operations in flight.
  • Transport: WebSocket for live pushes, HTTPS for bulk sync and admin APIs, mutual TLS with a certificate per device. Remote devices join a location's network over WireGuard through Tailscale (or self-hosted Headscale).
  • Cloud: containers on a managed service (Cloud Run, ECS or Fly.io), object storage for backups, receipts, photos and media.
  • Admin portal: the same Flutter app in a manager role on a Mac (or an iPad), same widgets as the POS, for owners and managers.

4Staying up

Every terminal holds a ranked list of backends and checks them every couple of seconds: primary hub, standby hub, main cloud region, backup region. Switching is automatic and shows as a status light, not a pop-up. Hubs and cloud speak the same protocol and merge the same way, so a terminal can move between them mid-order. The hubs and the cloud are not a main system and a spare: each alone is complete, both accept orders for a location at the same moment, and each hub keeps an always-on outbound connection to the cloud, so an operation taken on either side reaches the other within a second or two and the kitchen gets every ticket whichever side took it.

Two hubs, two regions

The standby hub keeps a live copy of the primary's database through streaming replication. If the primary stops responding for about five seconds the standby takes over. A third vote prevents both from acting as primary when they merely lose sight of each other: the cloud when the internet is up, a small witness device (a Raspberry Pi) when it is not. If both still accept orders for a moment, the operation log merges them without loss. The hubs sit in different spots on separate UPS units and circuits, so one spill or one tripped breaker cannot take out both. The cloud runs in a main and a backup region; the backup keeps a replica seconds behind, is promoted on failed health checks, and every hub and terminal already holds both addresses.

Printing with and without hubs

Printers are Ethernet devices with reserved IP addresses on the closed restaurant network, which the cloud cannot reach directly. The backend always renders the whole ticket to ESC/POS bytes; delivery is a dumb print relay with no business logic. With hubs up, the hub prints directly. With hubs down, each terminal tells the cloud which printer addresses it can reach, the cloud hands every job to one of them (wired stations first), and the job moves to the next terminal if that one does not confirm in a few seconds. Every job has an id and is kept until a printer accepts it, so nothing is lost or printed twice; the cash drawer kick travels the same way. Each station also has a backup printer: a ticket not acknowledged within a few seconds reprints there with a "reprinted from Wok" header. Kitchen screens are terminals and need no relay, and remote operators never touch printers.

Cards while offline. Card processing needs the internet. With the line down, readers use the processor's store-and-forward mode, capped per transaction and per day by a setting you control, with cash always available. Which readers support it, and on what terms, is confirmed in phase 0.

5Orders & sync

Terminals never overwrite an order; they append operations to it. Each operation carries an id, the device that made it, and a hybrid logical clock timestamp: wall-clock time corrected so ordering stays right even when a tablet's clock drifts. The current order is the replay of its operations in clock order, and merging two copies is the union of their operations. An add_line from a terminal carries only the customer's choices (the engine's LineRequest); the backend resolves it and appends the priced line as its own operation, so every screen shows the backend's price.

Who owns what. Orders, payments and time punches are created at the location and flow up; the cloud stores and reports, never edits. Menus, prices, staff and roles are edited in the cloud and flow down as numbered snapshots; a hub can run for days on its last one. The 86 board and table leases are local to the location and held by its hub, mirrored to the cloud for remote operators and the storefront. Customers and loyalty are cloud records, with recent and frequent customers cached on hubs; points earned offline post when the operation reaches the cloud. Every link keeps an outbox and a cursor, retries until acknowledged, and ignores duplicates, so a flaky connection can never double a charge or a line.

6Data model

Every record has a UUIDv7 id created where it was made, so terminals never wait for the server. Money is integer cents. Every row carries tenant_id and, where it applies, location_id: one cloud serves every location while each hub stores only its own. Order lines store the engine's full resolved line as JSON, so a later menu change never alters a past receipt or report.

Languages: unlimited, with fallback chains

Every name, description and label is a map from language tag to text (en, zh-Hans, zh-Hant, es, vi…) with no limit; Simplified and Traditional Chinese are separate languages, and the kitchen label keeps its own key. Every surface has an ordered list of languages set per location: POS screens, customer display, receipts (two languages side by side if wanted), kitchen tickets per station, storefront, kiosk, boards and text messages. Staff pick their own screen language; a customer's preferred language lives in their profile. The owner defines fallback chains per language (zh-Hant → zh-Hans → en, kitchen → zh-Hans → en); nothing assumes English. The engine walks the chain, returns the first text found and which language it came from, which feeds a translation gap report and a menu check warning per enabled language. Search covers every language at once, including pinyin (zzt finds 左宗棠鸡). The apps bundle fonts for every enabled script, handle right-to-left, and print a kitchen ticket as an image when a printer cannot render its script. The same chains apply to the apps' own buttons and labels.

Media: photos on anything

Photos and short videos attach to items, variants, options, categories and sections, combos, ingredients, recipes and their steps, stations and locations, several per thing, each with a purpose (menu photo, POS tile, plating reference, prep step, ingredient label, receiving photo, gallery), limited to channels and overridable per location. They show as POS photo tiles, on the storefront, kiosk, boards and customer display, in marketplace menus through the channel adapters, on the kitchen screen when a cook taps a ticket, and in the allergen review queue, where a label photo is the source on a declaration. Uploads come from the admin portal, a camera button on any terminal (manager approval before anything customer-facing), or the spreadsheet round-trip with a zip. The cloud strips camera metadata, makes every size once, and names files by content hash; the sizes a location uses travel with its menu snapshot and stay on its hubs, so tiles and plating photos work without internet and terminals fetch only what changed. Alt text per language, a usage-rights note, "where used" before deletion, and a missing-photo report per channel round it out. Two tables, media and media_attachment, join the design's nineteen; item_detail and menu_for return ready-to-use URLs per channel. Menu media is content with no retention limit, kept apart from the staff audit photos in section 10.

8Taxes

The engine attaches a tax class to every line; the order module decides what to charge. The model covers every continental state and the county, city and district taxes under them. Every rate, rule and exemption is data with an effective date, set per location.

How a check is taxed. Each line gets its class from item, variant, category or modifier, and the order carries its type, channel, location and customer. The location's rules pick a group or exempt, using the delivery zone where the location taxes at the destination. The base is the line after the discounts that reduce it (a store coupon does; a third-party-reimbursed coupon may not, by state). Each component is computed as a percentage of the base (or of base plus earlier components when compounding), a per-unit amount from the variant's size, or a fixed amount. Fees and charges go through the same rules with their own class, so a mandatory service charge or delivery fee is taxed only where the state taxes it; voluntary tips never are. Inclusive pricing, per menu, item or location, backs the tax out of the price. Rounding is per line or per check as the location sets, half up by default, and the result is written as tax lines.

Rates live on the hubs as well as in the cloud, so tax is identical offline. A rate service (Stripe Tax, Avalara or TaxJar) can look up rates by address, suggest updates and verify delivery addresses, but is never in the path of a sale. The places and rates here are examples of the model's range, not a tax guide. Each location's rules should be set up with its accountant and reviewed when rates change.

9Payments

The POS talks to a payment layer with one adapter per provider; each adapter starts, captures, tips, voids, refunds and reports status. Card data never enters the system: the reader talks to the processor, and the POS stores a reference, which keeps PCI scope minimal. Adding a provider is one adapter.

Routing to the lowest cost. When staff press Pay by card, a router on the hub keeps only providers that are reachable with a reader at this station, picks the lowest estimated fee for the amount from each provider's rate table (small tickets favor a low fixed fee, large ones a low percentage), applies your preferences (a default per location, a volume target), and names the reader or sends the payment to it. A provider that is down is skipped. The limit: once a card is tapped on a reader, that reader's provider processes it, so the cost choice happens when the reader is chosen. Bar tabs need pre-authorization, incremental authorization and capture with tip, and go only to providers whose readers support it.

What is recorded. Provider, reference, method (tap, chip, wallet, QR), estimated fee, and later the actual fee. Refunds and tip adjustments return to the original provider. A nightly reconciliation pulls each provider's payout reports and shows the real cost per provider, so the routing rules are proven or corrected. Orders from ezCater and other marketplaces arrive paid and are recorded as external payments with the channel's commission as a fee. Each provider has its own merchant account; keys stay in the cloud and on hubs, never on terminals.

10Identity & access

A terminal is enrolled once by a manager scanning a code and receives a certificate tied to one location; a lost device is revoked in the portal. The admin portal uses email sign-in with two-factor authentication. Staff sign in on an enrolled device three ways, and the device only collects; the backend decides who it is. Credentials and roles are cached on the hubs, so sign-in works without internet.

A photo with every major action

Every device with a front camera takes a quick low-resolution photo (about 320 pixels, 20 to 30 KB) of whoever is operating it when a major action is committed, and attaches it to that action in the audit log: creating an order, modifying or voiding a line, firing, discounts and comps, closing a check, refunds, drawer opens, paid in and out, clock in and out, manager approvals and 86 changes, each a switch per location. The action never waits for the camera; if it is covered or missing, the action goes through marked "no photo". Recent photos stay on the hubs, all go to cloud object storage (a busy day at 20 terminals is well under 100 MB), and a manager opens any refund and sees who was at the screen. An optional session guard compares each photo with the signed-in person's enrolled face and flags mismatches for review without blocking anything. Photos are deleted after 30 days by default, 90 at most, except those attached to a dispute. Customer-facing displays and kiosks never take photos, and a flipped screen's camera is off while it faces the customer.

Consent and the law

Face signatures are biometric data. Illinois requires written consent and a public retention policy and allows lawsuits; Texas and Washington have similar laws; California's privacy law covers employee data; New York requires written notice of electronic monitoring; Portland, Oregon restricts face recognition in places open to the public. So face sign-in and session guard are opt-in per location after a legal review for its state, and per person: enrollment records a signed consent with date and policy version, staff who decline use PIN or a tag with no difference in what they can do, signatures are encrypted, kept only on the location's hubs and the group's own cloud, never shared with a vendor, and deleted within 30 days of leaving or on withdrawal; enrollment photos are deleted once the signature exists. Event photos are plain photos but still monitoring: written notice at hiring, an on-screen indicator when a photo is taken, and viewing photos is itself an audited, manager-only action. One policy page in the portal lists everything collected about staff, why and for how long.

Not legal advice. Before face sign-in or event photos go live at a location, have an employment lawyer in that state check the consent form, the notice and the retention policy.

11Phones & remote operators

The restaurant's lines run on FreePBX (VoIP) hosted in the cloud, so remote staff answer on a softphone or desk phone exactly as on-site staff do. A PBX connector in the POS cloud subscribes to Asterisk's event feed (the Asterisk Manager Interface) over an encrypted connection, maps each extension to a staff member or terminal, and pops the caller's profile, past orders and usual order on the screen of whoever picks up. The same link gives click-to-call from a customer record, and FreePBX's caller ID lookup asks the POS for the customer's name so it shows on the phone. Calls and audio stay on FreePBX; if it is unreachable, calls follow its own fallback without the pop-up and the POS carries on.

Remote operators

Remote workers take phone and takeout orders from anywhere. Their device joins the location's network over WireGuard (Tailscale, or self-hosted Headscale), reaches only the hub's POS service, never printers or cameras, and talks to the hub like an on-site terminal, so orders print in the kitchen instantly. Three locks apply: the VPN, the device certificate, and the staff sign-in with a second factor at the start of each shift, optionally limited to set hours. One operator can be allowed into several locations. The remote role creates and edits phone and takeout orders, looks up customers, quotes wait times, and takes payment by text link or "pay at pickup"; no cash drawer, no voids after firing, no refunds. If the hubs cannot be reached for any reason (hubs down, VPN misconfigured, firewall change), the device switches to the cloud within seconds and the cloud forwards orders over the hub's own outbound link. Only when the restaurant's internet itself is down does the location show as unavailable, so no order is taken that the kitchen cannot see.

One call pool across locations

Each location has its own number, and each number's inbound route in FreePBX tags the call with its location (a prefix on the caller ID name, such as WS: Linda Chen, and a call variable the POS reads). Each location has its own queue: its own cashiers ring first, and if nobody answers within a set time or too many calls wait, the queue widens to cashiers at other locations and remote operators, using Asterisk queue priorities. A cashier taps Take calls to join; the POS pauses them while they ring up a customer at the counter and unpauses after, and signing out removes them. Managers choose which locations help which and during which hours, and a live board shows calls waiting per location, time to answer, and who is on a call where. A call on a number not tied to one location asks the operator to choose, suggesting the caller's usual location.

When the phone rings, a call card opens in the calling location's color; on answer, the whole order screen switches to that location's menu, prices, taxes, hours, 86 list and wait times, and the banner stays until the order is sent:

12Table service, sushi, bar and catering

All of this sits on the order model and the menu engine without new machinery; the extensions are the six listed in section 7.

Table service

  • Seats and courses. Every line carries a seat and a course (drinks, appetizers, entrées, dessert, custom). Tickets and the expo screen show both; a check splits by seat with one tap. Courses are held and fired by the server, after a set delay, or by the expo when the previous course is bumped; the kitchen screen shows what is held and for how long. Rush and VIP tags and the allergy note print on every station's copy.
  • Moving things. Transfer a table or check to another server, move or merge tables, combine checks, split by seat, item, amount or evenly, reopen a closed check with approval. Each is an operation, so it merges and audits like everything else.
  • Paying. At the table on a handheld, or by a QR code on the receipt that lets the guest pay and tip on their phone. Suggested tips on receipts and readers. Automatic gratuity above a party size, as a service charge with its own tax class. Tips adjustable until the nightly batch.
  • Cash. Server banking or cashier banking per location. The server close-out shows cash due, card tips, sales by category and open checks to close first.
  • Tips and payroll. Pooling by role and shift, tip-outs to bartenders, bussers and sushi chefs, card tip fees where passed on, declared cash tips at clock-out, a per-employee tip report for payroll and IRS reporting, and the ADP export.
  • Hosting. Floor plan with sections and server assignments, table status (open, ordered, entrées fired, check dropped, paid, needs bussing), turn times, a waitlist with text paging, and a built-in reservation book, with the Resy adapter in section 13.
  • Expo and runners. An expo mode shows every ticket, bumps per item, paces courses, and prints runner slips with table and seat.

Sushi bar

  • Shapes the engine already handles: nigiri by the piece (1 pc and 2 pc variants), half rolls, wrap choices (soy paper, no rice, cucumber) as substitutions on the rice role, platters and omakase as combos, chef's choice per guest with courses fired from the sushi station, sake by glass, carafe or bottle with hot or cold as a modifier.
  • Market and open price: a variant flagged market price takes today's price from the 86 board or portal; an open-price variant takes a typed price with approval rules. Both record on the line like any price.
  • The station: a kitchen screen or printer with all-day piece counts; counter seats are tables on the floor plan and the chef can ring in orders directly.
  • Fish by weight: cuts and yields in the recipes (loin to saku to slices) give true cost and usage, and auto-86 fires when a critical fish runs out.
  • Consumer advisory on items flagged raw or undercooked, on menus, storefront and receipts, in every enabled language.

Full bar

  • Tabs. Open by name or by card with a pre-authorization that grows with the tab and is captured with tip at close. Tabs transfer to a table and back, close in a row at last call, and unclosed tabs are captured at end of day under the location's default tip rule.
  • Drinks in the engine. Pour sizes as variants or scale modifiers; well, call, premium and top shelf as tiers on a spirit role, so "make it Grey Goose" swaps the ingredient and adds the upcharge; mixers and garnishes as modifiers; flights and pitchers as combos and variants; cocktail specs from the recipe on the bar screen.
  • Taps and kegs. Each tap maps to a keg of known size; pours deplete it; the bar screen shows ounces left and warns before a keg kicks; "keg kicked" on the 86 board swaps the next keg and updates the tap list on boards and online.
  • Routing by origin: drinks from servers print at the service bar, the bartender's own rings stay on their screen, and the main bar can watch the service bar queue.
  • Happy hour and last call. Happy hour is a scheduled menu; a location chooses whether an order started in happy hour keeps its prices. Alcohol service hours block alcohol lines outside legal hours, with a logged override.
  • Age checks. The first alcoholic item prompts: a tap to confirm or a license barcode scan that reads the birth date and keeps nothing else, logged with who checked.
  • Waste and inventory. A waste operation records spills and remakes with a reason and depletes inventory without a sale. Bottle counts by weight or tenths, pour cost and bottle variance arrive with inventory in phase 4.
  • Compliance reports: food versus alcohol sales by day and month for liquor license rules, plus comps, voids and spills by bartender.

Catering

  • The order. Event date and time, headcount, pickup or delivery with address and contact, setup notes, utensils and disposables, per-item lead times, quotes that become orders, change and cancellation cutoffs per location, every change an operation the kitchen sees.
  • Menus and money. A catering channel menu with half and full trays as variants, per-person items with a minimum headcount, packages as combos, packaging and utensils as recipe components. Deposits and partial payments, balance due at handover, invoices and receipts by email, house accounts with terms, service, delivery and gratuity charges with their own tax classes, delivery-zone tax where required.
  • Production and delivery. A daily prep sheet aggregates catering orders by item and, through the recipe explosion, by ingredient. Orders fire automatically ahead of the event; tray labels print with item names, allergens and customer; a packing list prints with the receipt; a driver is assigned and marks delivery with a photo in the driver app the design plans for.
  • ezCater orders arrive through the channel adapter in section 13 and behave exactly like orders taken by staff.

14Back office

  • Reporting. Reports run on a cloud read replica seconds behind, never on the database taking orders; cross-location analytics and years of history sit in a column store fed from it. Standard reports: sales by server, section, hour and location; product mix with food, beverage and catering split; voids and comps after firing by employee; discounts by reason; table turns and covers; ticket times per station; tip and tip-out summaries; cash variance per drawer or server; catering due this week with balances; tax by jurisdiction; food versus alcohol; and a daily close with each provider's batch beside the cash count. Owners get a daily summary by text or email at close.
  • Your data is yours. Every report exports to a spreadsheet, every table is reachable through a documented API under the same roles, and a full export is available on request.
  • Labor. Time clock, roles and tip reporting are built in because they are tied to sign-in; scheduling connects through the adapter in section 13 and could be built in later by replacing it. Meal and rest break reminders and attestations, overtime alerts before they happen, minor work-hour limits and predictive-scheduling notices are set per location.
  • Loyalty and gift cards. Points or visits, rewards redeemed as discounts the engine already handles, tiers, birthday and win-back offers. A gift card liability ledger in the cloud, physical cards and e-gifts, redeemable at every location. Points earned offline post when the order reaches the cloud; redemptions offline are allowed up to a set amount and settle on reconnect.
  • Marketing and privacy. Text and email campaigns from the CRM with consent per channel and customer, opt-out everywhere, quiet hours respected. Customer data encrypted, access logged, and a customer's request to see or delete their data handled from one screen.
  • Customer-facing screens. A customer display at the register shows the order, prompts for tip and signature, offers a text or email receipt and loyalty sign-up by phone number, and never takes photos. Tap lists, specials and menu boards run on the menu engine, in the location's languages, and change the moment a price or an 86 changes.
  • Inventory and purchasing (phase 4). Counts by storage area, par levels and suggested orders, purchase orders and vendor invoices that update costs with invoice photos, transfers between locations, waste logs, and theoretical versus actual usage with variance by ingredient, on the ingredient master the design already shares.

15Operations & quality

  • Updates that never interrupt service. Releases go to a test location first, then to hubs in waves outside service hours, standby before primary. The app and backend speak a versioned API, so older apps keep working and terminals update when idle. Any release rolls back per location. New features switch on per location behind flags.
  • Hubs as appliances. One standard image: plug in, scan a code, and the hub enrolls, pulls its location's data and joins as standby. Replacing a dead hub takes ten minutes. Hubs patch themselves in the maintenance window and are the time source for every device on the network.
  • Watching everything. Every hub, terminal, printer, reader, VPN link and region reports health to a status board, with text and email alerts: hub failed over, printer out of paper, sync behind by a minute, provider failing, certificate expiring. Logs and metrics collect centrally; a diagnostic bundle is one tap away.
  • Backups and recovery. Every operation reaches the cloud within seconds; the cloud database is replicated to the second region with point-in-time recovery, backups kept a year, media and receipts versioned in object storage, a restore rehearsed quarterly. Targets: seconds of data at risk at most, hub takeover in about five seconds, region takeover in minutes.
  • Security. Card data never enters the system (simplest PCI category; readers are the processors' certified devices). A closed POS network per location, device certificates, encrypted disks on hubs, secrets in a vault, encryption at rest and in transit, a yearly penetration test, and audit logs for every data access by staff and support.
  • Support. Support reaches hubs over the VPN and sees a terminal's screen with the operator's consent, never through an opened port. A "help" button sends a diagnostic bundle and opens a ticket with location and device filled in.
  • Day-to-day details. A business day ends at a set hour (say 4 am) so a 1 am tab lands on the right night in every report and batch. Training mode on any terminal runs the full system with nothing posted, printed or charged, under a watermark. Opening a location is a portal checklist: create it, copy menus and tax rules from a similar one, image two hubs, enroll devices by code, discover printers, set languages, run printer and payment tests, go live, in about a day.
  • Testing and rollout. The engine and order model are tested like a calculator: the design's worked examples and property tests, plus operation logs replayed out of order, duplicated and with pulled cables, on every change. A simulated restaurant of twenty virtual terminals, kitchen screens and printers runs a scripted Friday night against a real hub and the cloud, with hubs killed and the internet cut mid-service, as the release gate. A hardware lab holds one of every device, reader and printer model. One real location, or a test tenant, gets every release first. Phase 1 ends with a shadow week beside the old system, totals compared nightly.

Every row carries a tenant id, so the same cloud could host another restaurant group with full separation. Nothing depends on it; it costs nothing now.

16Hardware & network

  • Hubs: two fanless mini PCs (8 GB RAM, SSD, Linux), each on its own small UPS in different spots, about $300 to $500 each, plus a small witness device (a Raspberry Pi) for failover votes.
  • Printers: Ethernet ESC/POS printers (Epson TM series or compatible) with reserved IP addresses, one per kitchen station plus a backup, a receipt printer with cash drawer at each register. USB-only printers do not fit, since nothing could reach them when their host is down.
  • Fixed stations: iPads or Android tablets on stands, or a Mac mini with a touch screen, with the front camera (or a USB webcam) and a USB NFC reader.
  • Handhelds: iPhones, iPad minis or Android handhelds with a paired card reader for pay at the table; iPhones and most Android handhelds read NFC tags on their own.
  • NFC tags: fobs, cards or phone stickers (NTAG 424 DNA or DESFire), $1 to $3 each.
  • Kitchen and bar screens (KDS): iPads or Android tablets in wall mounts, or a Mac mini driving a large display, one per station plus expo.
  • Card readers: one from each provider you want to route between at the stations where it matters, or one default reader everywhere with the others as backup.
  • Network: a separate POS network, wired for hubs, stations and printers, Wi-Fi for handhelds, a cellular backup on the router. At 20 terminals a busy Friday is a few thousand operations an hour, well within one small hub.

18Risks

  • Terminals depend on a backend. Screens stop only if both hubs and the internet fail together; two hubs, two regions and a cellular backup make that rare.
  • Sync bugs are costly. The merge logic is one small package with heavy tests, including random disconnect and reorder, on every change.
  • Provider depth. Not every provider lets a third-party POS fully drive its readers; one that does not can still be used as a standalone reader with the amount keyed in, losing automatic routing for it. Desktop SDKs are avoided by server-driven readers. Offline store-and-forward risks later declines, so limits are configurable.
  • Marketplace access. If ezCater or Resy do not offer direct partner access, an aggregator carries them; the adapter shape is the same.
  • Biometric law. Face sign-in is opt-in per location and person with consent records, in-house encrypted signatures and scheduled deletion; a location where it is not allowed runs on PIN and tags, nothing else changed.
  • Tax settings. Effective-dated rates, a weekly collected-versus-expected report per jurisdiction, and each location's rules reviewed with its accountant.
  • Printers and scripts. Chinese character support varies; tickets fall back to printing as an image on any ESC/POS printer.
